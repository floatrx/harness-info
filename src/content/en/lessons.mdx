Each lesson below cost real time in a harness that ran for weeks. They are written as a problem, what it looked
like from the outside, and the rule that came out of it. Read the rule if you read nothing else.

## Closure is a property of the plan, not of the loop

**Problem.** Every task had many acceptance criteria, and every one of them needed proof before the task could close.

**What it looked like.** The loop was busy for hours and the board filled up with tasks that were almost done. Almost
nothing closed.

**Rule.** Tag every criterion `[closes]`, `[found]` or `[process]`, and let only `[closes]` decide closure. Aim for
one `[closes]` per task. The gate shrank several times over and tasks started to finish.

## Measure work, not one component's way of recording it

**Problem.** Progress was counted as new commits. Some units do real work without committing: they write a handoff
for the next unit.

**What it looked like.** A healthy run was stopped as "barren" while it was moving forward.

**Rule.** Progress is commits **or** handoffs. Count every kind of output the loop produces.

## Verify every reading against the thing it claims to measure

**Problem.** Numbers on the live view were trusted because they looked plausible.

**What it looked like.** Three different readings turned out to measure something other than their label.

**Rule.** Before you rely on a number, check it once by hand against the thing it claims to measure.

## Unattended work has to be observable, or nobody trusts it

**Problem.** The loop ran, but the only way to know what it was doing was to read files.

**What it looked like.** Nobody let it run overnight, because nobody could see whether it was working or stuck.

**Rule.** Build the watcher first, before the loop has much to show. Trust follows visibility.

## A turn that ends waiting is a turn that ends

**Problem.** Sub-agent calls in the CLI are asynchronous, and in `claude -p` a message without a tool call ends the turn.

**What it looked like.** The coordinator dispatched its specialists, wrote "waiting for results", and the unit ended.
The answers arrived later and went to nobody.

**Rule.** Dispatch, then hold the turn with tool calls until every specialist has reported. Back it with the trace
contract. Details on the [coordination](/coordination) page.

## An observer measures the process that works, not the one waiting for it

**Problem.** The watcher judged a unit by its coordinator's activity.

**What it looked like.** While specialists worked, the coordinator was quiet for minutes, and the watcher reported the
unit as stuck.

**Rule.** Watch the sub-agents too. A quiet coordinator is not a stuck unit.

## A progress bar whose cell is wider than a unit looks stopped

**Problem.** The progress bar moved one cell per finished unit, and a unit can take a long time.

**What it looked like.** The bar sat still for long stretches and people assumed the run had died.

**Rule.** Show motion inside the unit: its agents, its current tool. Judge elapsed time against the measured range of
past units, not against a guess.

## A question shown beside live rows is not seen

**Problem.** An agent's question to the human appeared as one more row next to busy live output.

**What it looked like.** Questions waited for hours, and the task waited with them.

**Rule.** A question that blocks work takes the whole frame. Make it impossible to miss.

## A lexical shortcut is not a judgement

**Problem.** To save tokens, a judgement that needed understanding was replaced by a word-overlap check.

**What it looked like.** Items that meant the same thing in different words did not match, and items that shared words
but meant different things did.

**Rule.** If the decision needs a model, use a model. Do not replace a judgement with string matching.

## A contract that stops the damage still owes you the reason

**Problem.** A contract stopped a bad unit correctly, but said only that a check had failed.

**What it looked like.** Every stop meant digging through logs to find out what had happened.

**Rule.** Every gate message names the likely cause and the next step.

## Never kill processes by pattern

**Problem.** An agent tried to stop one of its own helper processes with a pattern.

**What it looked like.** `pkill -f` matches the pattern anywhere in any full command line, so a short word matched
almost every running app, including other Claude sessions.

**Rule.** Stop only a process you started, by the exact PID you captured. This rule is in the standing rules of every
agent.
